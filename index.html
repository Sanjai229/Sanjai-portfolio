01 In progress · delivery Nov 2026

AI-Powered Startup Assessment Platform

Capstone project with an industry partner - a website where founders upload their pitch deck or business plan and get back a report and a score

The Problem

Startup accelerators and investors get far more applications than they can read properly. Going through them by hand is slow, and two reviewers often score the same pitch differently.

Our industry partner asked us to build a platform where a founder uploads a pitch deck, business plan or summary, and gets back a written assessment and a readiness score - based on the same things a human reviewer would look for.

My Role

I'm the Scrum Master for a team of five. I plan our sprints in Jira and help run the weekly meeting with the client.

On the technical side, I build the part that reads the uploaded files. It takes PDFs, slide decks and plain text, pulls out the sections and numbers, and passes clean data to the scoring step. Getting useful data out of documents that people wrote by hand is the hardest part of the whole thing.

How It Works

Every founder writes their documents differently, so there is no fixed layout to rely on. My code tidies them all into one shared format.

The scoring step then uses LLMs to check each submission against set criteria and write a short report with a number attached. We build in sprints and agree what to do next at the weekly client meeting, rather than planning everything upfront.

What I'm Learning

Building for a client is very different from a university assignment. The scope changes, finishing on time matters more than making it perfect, and the client cares whether the score makes sense - not which model produced it.

Being Scrum Master has also shown me how much of the work is simply keeping people moving in the same direction.

02

E-Commerce Sales & Delivery Analysis in SQL

100,000 real orders analysed in MySQL - finding out why a fast-growing business quietly stopped growing

The Problem

This dataset holds about 100,000 online orders from a Brazilian marketplace, spread across nine tables and two years of trading.

Instead of just practising SQL, I asked the questions a real analyst gets asked: where does the money come from, do deliveries arrive when promised, what makes customers leave bad reviews, and do they ever come back?

What I Did

I built the database myself in MySQL 8 - choosing the column types and indexing the keys used for joins. Then I loaded over 500,000 rows with LOAD DATA INFILE, turning blank dates into NULL as they loaded so the date calculations stayed correct.

After that I wrote 16 queries. They use joins across tables, CTEs, CASE WHEN and HAVING, plus window functions - LAG, LEAD, ROW_NUMBER, RANK and NTILE - along with running totals and moving averages.

What I Found

Late orders average 2.57 stars, on-time orders 4.29. Over half of all late orders get 1 or 2 stars. Only 2,997 of roughly 96,000 customers ever ordered a second time.

Problems I Hit

- Hidden line-break characters from Windows CSV files were stuck to every category name. You couldn't see them, but they would have broken every filter later on.
- The first and last months were incomplete, which made growth look like +697,878%.
- customer_id is a new value for every order, not one per person. Using it would have made repeat customers look far higher than they really are.
- I tested whether heavier products get worse reviews. It only held true at the extremes, so I dropped the idea.

What I Learned

Revenue in 2018 looked healthy at about $1M a month. But when I compared each month to the one before, growth was close to zero. The raw numbers hid it - it only showed up once I calculated the change.

The findings link together. Growth stalled because only 3% of customers came back, while late deliveries were actively pushing people away. This is observational data, so I can't prove cause and effect. The write-up explains how I would test it properly.

03

LLM-based eCommerce Application

Three AI agents for an online phone store - writing ads, recommending products, and making sense of customer reviews

The Problem

An online phone store has hundreds of product photos, 720 products and nearly 68,000 customer reviews.

Tagging photos by hand or reading reviews one by one doesn't scale, and SQL can't understand written English. So I built three LLM agents to handle the whole job - from writing the ads to working out what customers actually think.

How It Works

Agent 1 - BLIP2 looks at a product photo and describes it. Qwen2-VL then writes a slogan in a chosen tone, and PIL places it on the image.

Agent 2 - TableLLM-7B answers questions typed in plain English. Qwen3-1.7B gives fuller recommendations through a simple Gradio web page.

Agent 3 - Qwen3-4B sorts every review into Positive, Neutral or Negative, pulls out the main themes, and suggests product improvements based on what customers said.

Problems I Hit

- BLIP2's tokenizer didn't work with Python 3.12
- My regex kept picking up punctuation along with the numbers in price filters
- TableLLM was more accurate, but Qwen3 wrote better English, so I had to choose model by task
- Running all 67,986 reviews without running out of GPU memory

What I Learned

The model has to match the job. BLIP2 is very good at describing images but poor at writing creatively.

The bigger lesson: LLMs can handle vague, everyday language that SQL simply cannot. And one Qwen3-4B model did the sorting, the summarising and the suggestions all in a single pass.

04

Delivery Performance Dashboard in Power BI

Turning the SQL findings into a one-page dashboard that tells leadership where to act, not just what happened

The Problem

Project 02 found that late deliveries push customers into leaving bad reviews. But a finding written in a report doesn't get acted on.

A manager doesn't have time to read a page of numbers. They need to open one screen and immediately see which states to deal with first. So I rebuilt the analysis as a dashboard.

How I Built It

I wrote a SQL query that flattens nine tables into one row per order, with the lateness, review score and order value already worked out. That gave me 96,470 clean rows to load into Power BI.

In Power BI I removed duplicates, cut the incomplete months, and wrote DAX measures for on-time rate, bad review rate and revenue at risk. A calculated column groups every order into a lateness bucket.

São Paulo alone has $1.56M of revenue sitting on bad reviews. Maranhão is a smaller state but 23.1% of its orders arrive late.

Design Decisions

The main table only lists states with enough orders to be meaningful, sorted by money at risk. Anything above an 8% late rate turns red, so the eye goes straight to the problem rows.

The bar chart goes green to red as orders get later. You can read the whole story without looking at a single number.

What I Learned

An experienced BI manager gave me the advice that shaped this: move from "interesting to know" to "here is what to do". My first instinct was to chart everything. Instead, every visual on the page has to survive one question - what would someone do differently after seeing this?

Anything that didn't answer it got cut.
